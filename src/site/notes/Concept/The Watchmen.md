## Introduction

This study examines the **function of the watchman** — not just the ancient lookout but the office then and now: what need is *being fulfilled*, and the relationships that define this role. Three run through every category below:

- **The watchman and the people** — the watchman is set *over* them and *for* them, seeing on their behalf and answering for their souls (Ezekiel 33:2–6; Isaiah 62:6).
- **The watchman and the function** — the office exists to see, to warn, to keep watch and never keep silent; it is defined by its charge, and measured by it (Isaiah 62:6; Ezekiel 3:17).
- **The watchmen among themselves** — never a lone figure but a company on the walls, who "lift up their voices, they shout joyfully **together**" (Isaiah 52:8); the watch is shared, and so, in the end, is the rest (Nehemiah 4:16–22).

The verse that gathers most of this is Isaiah 62:6–7 — *"On your walls, O Jerusalem, I have appointed watchmen... they will never keep silent... and give Him no rest until He establishes and makes Jerusalem a praise in the earth."* Watchmen *on the walls* (function), *for Jerusalem* (the people), who *give Him no rest* (their bond to the Lord). The watchmen never stand alone — Isaiah 52:8 sets them "together" on the wall: one watch made of many, so that the seeing and the warning are carried in common, and no stretch of wall is left unwatched.

## A · The watchman on the wall / tower (narrative)
The lookout — the picture to be built upon.

> [!note]- Helpful notes
> **The trained eye.** The watchman's first skill is *identification* — seeing what is incoming before anyone else, and knowing what it means. He is trained on cues: a lone runner means tidings (2 Samuel 18:24–26), and he can name a man by his gait alone — "the running of the first one is like the running of Ahimaaz the son of Zadok" (2 Samuel 18:27) — or a driver by his driving: "the driving is like the driving of Jehu... for he drives furiously" (2 Kings 9:20). Trained to identify threat, to see the incoming, to read the signs given him — and he must be *well-trained* to be effective. An untrained eye on the wall is as useless as a blind one (Isaiah 56:10, category C).
- 2 Samuel 18:24–27 — the watchman over the gate sees Ahimaaz running
- 2 Samuel 13:34 — "the young man who was the watchman raised his eyes and looked" — Show the function of a warning of things outside the wall. First to see the change.
- 1 Samuel 14:16 — Saul's watchmen in Gibeah see the multitude melting away — the watchmen see the works the Lord has done in the enemy's camp first
- 2 Kings 9:17–20 — the watchman on the tower in Jezreel sights Jehu's approach. (Side note: This is where a prophet is given a part of the word but when he spoke the rest of the words came out)
- Isaiah 21:6, 8 — "Go, station the lookout... on the watchtower I stand continually by day" The watchmen/ lookout hear the command of the LORD of hosts, and make it known to the congregation (they are part of the troop that make sure the army is organized instep with the Lord)

#### The longer explanation
> *I hope you have studied the word above — in context — before reading this; you will get far more out of it.*

Think of a sniper. Sometimes the sniper works with a **spotter** — a dedicated lookout beside the shooter; sometimes the sniper is the lookout, reading the field through the scope with the rifle in hand. Either way the shooter must judge **distance** accurately and *fast*: is that figure at 50 feet, 100, 200, 300? A precision weapon leaves no room to guess, so the eye is trained on **cues**. The face is one of them: up close it is plain, but detail falls off with distance — to the naked eye, facial identification drops to nearly zero around **150 feet** (not 50 or 100, where a face is still clear; by about 200 feet you can no longer make out the eyes). So the shooter learns exactly what to look for at each range, and reads the distance quickly from those details.

This is the very skill the watchmen carry. They are **highly trained** — they know precisely what to look for, and can name it: a man by the way he runs (2 Samuel 18:27), a driver by the way he drives the chariots (2 Kings 9:20), an army's condition by the stir in its camp (1 Samuel 14:16). The little things are the whole craft; an untrained eye sees a smudge, the trained watchman sees the details — *who*, *what*, and *how far*.

But the discipline opens into a gift. Because the watchman is set to look at the right place, the watchman is the first to **see the work of the Lord** — the enemy host melting away before a blow is struck (1 Samuel 14:16), the deliverer riding in (2 Kings 9:17–20). The one stationed to watch is the one who sees God move before anyone else does.

And the watch is not passive. In Isaiah 21:6, 8 the lookout stands at the tower *"continually by day"* and reports what the LORD of hosts — the LORD of the armies — shows. The watchman is set like a forward element of the army itself, part of the troop, relaying the commander's word to the ranks. The task is not merely to pay attention but to **order the line in the time of war**, keeping the army in step with the LORD of hosts. That was the office in the natural; it is the office still — and we are at war.

## B · The prophet as watchman — the charge and the blood-guilt
The heart of the calling: accountability.
- Ezekiel 3:16–21 — "I have appointed you a watchman for the house of Israel" *(the fuller unit, not just v. 17)*

> [!info]- Context
> **Setting — Chebar, the great river (Ezekiel 1:1, 3).** *Chebar* carries *great* — the great canal. By this river the glory of the LORD appeared to Ezekiel (Ezekiel 1; and returned to him there, Ezekiel 43:3) **before** any charge was given. This is the pattern: **you receive your commission beside the water, in the presence of the Lord** — vision before sending (so Daniel too receives his vision by the great river, Daniel 10:4; and the Son is sealed at the Jordan before He is sent, Matthew 3:16–17). What the watchman later warns, he warns as one who has already beheld the glory.

> [!info]- Context
> **Setting — Tel-abib, the mound with two names (Ezekiel 3:15).** The name says two things at once — *til abūbi*, the **mound of the flood** (the desolation, the present condition, what the eye sees), and to the Hebrew ear *tēl-abib*, the **mound of spring**, the green ears of grain (the firstfruits, what the Lord has for the place). **This is how the Lord speaks to His people:** He sees the ruin and the harvest *together* — and the watchman must see both as He does, not one after the other but at once. *(Philology and sources: [[Til Abubi and Tel-abib\|Til Abubi and Tel-abib]])* 

- Ezekiel 33:1–9 — the watchman passage; the blood-guilt lands at v. 6: "his blood I will require from the watchman's hand"
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> **The blood is required.** If the watchman sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet, the people die in their sin — "but his blood I will require from the watchman's hand" (Ezekiel 33:6). Warn them and you are clean; stay silent and their blood is on your head (Ezekiel 33:8–9). To *see* and not *tell* is itself the sin. (Same chapter: the righteous who turns to injustice dies; the wicked who repents lives — Ezekiel 33:12–19.)
- Jeremiah 6:17 — "I set watchmen over you, saying, 'Listen to the sound of the trumpet!'"
> Prophet and watchman — equivalent here *(the overlap is drawn out in the longer explanation)*.
- Hosea 9:8 — "Ephraim was a watchman with my God, a prophet"
> [!info]- Context
> Ephraim here is the whole northern kingdom — interchangeable with Israel throughout Hosea. Gilgal had been the place of covenant beginnings: the first camp in the land, where the LORD "rolled away the reproach of Egypt" (Joshua 5:9). It has become the headquarters of apostasy — "All their evil is at Gilgal... I will drive them out of My house" (Hosea 9:15) — corrupt altar and corrupt throne together, for this is where the monarchy was made (1 Samuel 11:15), and "They have set up kings, but not by Me" (Hosea 8:4). Over such an Ephraim the prophet-watchman is set, and opposed: "a trapper's snare in all his ways, and hostility in the house of his God" (Hosea 9:8).
- Isaiah 21:11–12 — "Watchman, how far gone is the night?"
> [!info]- Context
> Dumah means *silence* — the condition the people cry out of: heaven shut, the night unlifting. They seek the watchman to learn when judgment ends. His answer, "Morning comes but also night," half turns them away — he affirms a dawn but withholds the hour, because the word and the judgment are still the Lord's; the watchman keeps the watch but does not set the morning. So "come back again" points past himself to the One who holds the night. Men seek the prophet in the time of trouble — if only they had sought the Lord before it.
- Habakkuk 2:1 — "I will stand at my guard post... to keep watch" — He speaks to the Lord
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> The watchman who turns his post around — keeping watch not for the enemy but "to see what He will speak to me" (Habakkuk 2:1). This is the small hinge where **the watchman needs a runner**: the vision is "inscribed on tablets, that the one who reads it may run" (Habakkuk 2:2) — the watchman receives the word and hands it to the runner.

#### The longer explanation
> *I hope you have studied the word above — in context — before reading this; you will get far more out of it.*

**Watchman and prophet — and a whole people called.** There is a consistent pattern here, and the direction matters: the **prophets are called to be watchmen** — the Lord appoints His prophet as a watchman (Ezekiel 3:17; 33:7; Hosea 9:8, "Ephraim was a watchman with my God, a prophet") — but we don't see it the other way around. And it isn't that the watchman always holds the *office* of the prophet, the fivefold prophet of Ephesians 4:11; rather, the watchman carries the same **function** as the prophet — to tell people what he sees. So this is not the fivefold office, yet it is the prophetic call. (This is to be frank a *personal conviction*)

Then notice the two levels. It is the **function of Ephraim** to be a watchman unto the people — and Ephraim has many functions within itself, so that it works together as one to become the watchman unto the nations. There is a distinction between a **person** called as a watchman and a **nation** called as a watchman. When a person is called, that call is an office he carries. When a nation is called, that too is an office — but within it are many people and many functions, all working so that the nation as a whole functions as the watchman.

And this is us. We, the church, are called to be the watchman of the world — to warn it of the wrong it is doing. Yet within the body there are many functions: some are watchmen, some are teachers, some are runners, some carry still other gifts — and each does its part so that the church itself becomes the watchman for the nations (Exodus 19:6; 1 Peter 2:9). In this sense, the watch was never meant for a lone few — it's a call for many, working as one. 

Yet, let's concern ourselves with the watchmen in the individual sense.

**So what is the requirement to stand in this place? First, the water.** We saw that Ezekiel was set near the river Chebar — the great river, the great canal — and there, before any charge, he met with the Lord. Before you can receive the commission, you have to find the water: the source of the great water, the living water Jesus spoke of, the water that never goes away and never leaves you thirsting again (John 4:14). That is the place where you are sealed. That is the place where you are sent, for His glory. You receive your command next to the water.

**Then comes the training — you have to learn how the Lord speaks.** Consider the context behind this place. By now the Jews had been torn out of Jerusalem — carried away from the city, the temple, the place of God's presence — and deported to Babylon, resettled far from home by the Chebar canal at a ruin-mound called Tel-abib (2 Kings 24:14–16; Ezekiel 1:1–3). It is there, in exile, that Ezekiel sat in the same place for seven days (Ezekiel 3:15) — looking, waiting, meditating — and was made a watchman. Even the name of that place carries a meaning: Tel-abib shows *how the Lord speaks* — and learning how He speaks is part of your charge over the people, so that you can tell them the things He is saying to you. For He speaks over both at once: the present condition — the flood, the place of desolation — and also the spring — the green ears, the firstfruits, the place and the things He has for them. He speaks both, and He speaks them continually.

And this is not just theory — we have seen it ourselves. As we pray for people, without fail the Lord does this very thing: He shows us where they are — their present condition, the desolation, the flood they are standing in — and in the same breath He shows us where they are *called to be* — the green ears, the firstfruits, the place He has prepared for them to walk into and continue in. Every time, both together. So as a watchman you have to learn to see and hear the Lord's voice this way, and to speak it back exactly as He gives it — the ruin as it is, and the field it is to become.

**The next step is to speak — and here is the requirement.** Hearing is not the end. Most of the time, once you have heard, you have to speak out, because there is a requirement laid on the office: the Lord wants all to turn back to Him. So the watchman has to be the voice of the Lord, warning people to turn back and away from their sin. For if the watchman does not warn, the sinner will die in his sin — yet the Lord will require that blood from the watchman's hand, because he did not warn, because that is his office (Ezekiel 33:6–8). We have to understand the weight of this office.

**And there will be times the people do not listen — and then they come to inquire of you.** This is Isaiah 21. When the people finally come asking, the watchman's condition has not changed: you keep watching — even through the night, watching and watching and watching — and you still speak of what you see. But there are times you are not there to comfort the people; you are only to tell them the present condition of what you see. You are not to twist it into what they want to hear. Lest you become the soothsayer, you say **exactly what is there, exactly what the Lord has given you.**

**The watchman — like Habakkuk — may bring their questions to the Lord.** The watchman stands in a place to speak to the Lord: to ask, to press for more, and to have Him reveal more. Habakkuk did exactly this — he brought the Lord hard, honest questions: *how long must I cry out?*, and even *how can You use a nation more wicked than us to judge us?* (Habakkuk 1). At first that can look like defiance — but it was not. He brought his questions *to* God, not against Him; then he took his post to wait for the answer (Habakkuk 2:1), and when it came, he obeyed it. So here is the line to hold: **asking is not doubting, and questioning is not disobeying.** Your job is not to doubt — your job is still to obey. You may inquire of the Lord anything, but you do so in faith, you wait for His word, and as He tells you, you do (even when you don't know why sometimes).

> [!warning]- Disclaimer — Test the spirit
> To obey even when you don't know why is never a licence to act on any voice. There is a world of difference between not understanding the **reason** of the Lord and not understanding the **source** it comes from. To be unable to test a spirit, and then say *I did it because it was of the Lord, even though I had no understanding* — that is wrong. Always test the spirit: "do not despise prophetic utterances, but examine everything carefully; hold firmly to that which is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21), and "do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God" (1 John 4:1). Keep the two apart: **not knowing the *will* of God — because He has not revealed it, and asks your obedience — calls for faith; but not being able to recognise the *voice* of God at all calls for testing, never blind action.**

**And one more thing about Habakkuk — the runner.** After Habakkuk received the vision, he was told to write it down and hand it to a runner, and the runner would run and carry the message (Habakkuk 2:2). So there are times when your shofar is enough — you sound the alarm yourself and those who are close listen, and that is sufficient. And it reaches further than that: from your stretch of the wall you can signal across to another watchman on his stretch, and he in turn warns the people on his side of the camp — one watch passing the word along to the next. (We will open this up more when we come to Nehemiah.) But there are also times you need a runner to send the message — when it is more complicated, or when it is something new that a trumpet-blast cannot carry. Either way, part of the reason is this: you have to stay on the watch — you cannot leave your post. So the runner carries what the watchman cannot leave to deliver.

## C · Watchmen of the city — faithful and failed
The contrast: watchmen who cry out vs. watchmen who are blind or who wound.
- Isaiah 62:6 — "On your walls, O Jerusalem, I have appointed watchmen" — charged to give themselves no rest, and to give God no rest either, until He makes Zion a praise (Isaiah 62:6–7).
- Isaiah 52:8 — "your watchmen lift up their voices, they shout joyfully together" — they break into song because they see, with their own eyes, the LORD returning to restore Zion.
- Isaiah 56:10 — "his watchmen are blind, all of them know nothing" *(the failed watchman)* — no better than lazy shepherds; and because none keeps watch, the wild beasts of the field are summoned in to devour the flock (Isaiah 56:9).
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> The mirror of Isaiah 52:8: the faithful watchman *sees* the LORD restore Zion and *shouts* (Isaiah 52:8; cf. Isaiah 62:1, 6 — never silent, taking no rest); the failed one is *blind, mute, asleep, self-serving* — "blind... mute dogs unable to bark... who love to slumber... greedy... each to his unjust gain" (Isaiah 56:10–11). The type recurs: blinded seers (Isaiah 29:10), the blind servant (Isaiah 42:19–20), watchmen who won't guard the breach (Ezekiel 13:5), prophets crying "Peace, peace" over a doomed city (Jeremiah 6:14; 8:11), fed prophets (Micah 3:5–7), the NT "blind guides" (Matthew 15:14; 23:16).
- Song of Solomon 3:3 — "the watchmen who make the rounds in the city found me"
- Song of Solomon 5:7 — the same watchmen "found me, they struck me and wounded me"
> *(I would study this out more)*
- Jeremiah 31:6 — "the watchmen on the hills of Ephraim call out, 'Arise, let us go up to Zion'" *(study further — 1 Kings 12:26–33; Hosea 8:4; 9:15; Jeremiah 31:2, 31–34)*
- Jeremiah 51:12 — "post a strong guard, station the watchmen" *(the watch set against Babylon)*
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> The Medes set their army and watchmen to strike Babylon — echoing His word at Jeremiah's call, "I am watching over My word to perform it" (Jeremiah 1:12). Behind the human watch stands God as the true Watchman over His word; no city watch holds against His purpose.
>
> The chapter's other pole: while the siege closes on Babylon, Israel is summoned to flee and rejoice — "Flee from the midst of Babylon, and each of you save his life!" (Jeremiah 51:6); "The LORD has brought about our vindication; come, let us recount in Zion the work of the LORD our God" (Jeremiah 51:10; cf. Jeremiah 51:45, 50). They rejoice in the deliverance, yet they must depart, strengthen, and re-establish — the returned kingdom's own watch still has to be set (compare Nehemiah 4:9; 7:3 below).
- Micah 7:4 — "The day when you post your watchmen, your punishment will come"
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> "The day of your watchmen" (root *tsaphah*, to look out) carries two understandings at once. **First** — the day the watchmen *foretold*: the day the prophets warned of, now arrived, the vindicated warning landing as judgment. **Second** — the day of your *looking*: the day you finally lift your eyes and watch, only to see your condition is already under judgment — hence "their confusion" (Micah 7:4b), the bewilderment of the caught, not the calm of the forewarned. The same day, foretold and finally-seen. Three verses on, the passage turns to the hopeful watch: "I will watch expectantly for the LORD" (Micah 7:7, category D). One text, both poles.
- Nehemiah 4:9; 7:3 — Nehemiah sets a guard/watch on the wall day and night
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> Nehemiah is the **restoration watch** — the returned people rebuilding under threat, rejoicing yet armed:
> - **Prayer joined to watch** — "we prayed to our God, and... set up a guard against them day and night" (Nehemiah 4:9); vigilance and intercession together (cf. Matthew 26:41, category F).
> - **Build with a weapon in hand** — half carry spears while half build, each with his sword girded at his side (Nehemiah 4:16–18); rejoice and rebuild, yet stay armed (the "rejoice yet strengthen" of Jeremiah 51 above).
> - **The trumpet rally** — "wherever you hear the sound of the trumpet, rally to us there; our God will fight for us" (Nehemiah 4:20); the watchman's trumpet gathering the people (cf. Ezekiel 33:3–6; Jeremiah 6:17).
> - **Unbroken vigilance** — none removed their clothes, each keeping his weapon even at the water (Nehemiah 4:23); the watch that never undresses (cf. Revelation 16:15, category F).
> - **A distributed watch** — "each at his post, and each in front of his own house" (Nehemiah 7:3); every inhabitant made a guard (the universal watch of Mark 13:37, category F).
> - **The watch guards the rest** — Nehemiah later stations guards at the gates to keep the Sabbath holy (Nehemiah 13:19–22); the watch protecting the day of rest.
> - **All watch, all build — so all may rest.** The labour and the watch are shared across the whole people: half guard while half build, and each lodges in the city to be "a guard by night and a labourer by day" (Nehemiah 4:16, 22). Because every man is ready and every man is at his post (Nehemiah 7:3), the watch is never dropped — and so each can take his rest in turn. Corporate readiness is what makes corporate rest possible: all are ready so that all, in their time, may enter the rest (Hebrews 4:9–11, "let us... enter that rest").

#### The longer explanation
> *I hope you have studied the word above — in context — before reading this; you will get far more out of it.*

**The watchman inside the city.** Here the watchman is set *inside* the city, inside the kingdom of God, and the function is the same as before: to be the one who speaks of what he sees. That is the whole of it — he sees, and he says.

**The joy — to see the works of the Lord first.** And there is a real joy in it: the watchman is the one who sees the works of the Lord *first*. When the LORD moves to restore Zion, it is the watchmen on the wall who see it and break into song (Isaiah 52:8). To be stationed to watch is to be the first to behold what God is doing — and that is an amazing thing.

**The failure — to see and not speak.** But when the watchman sees what he ought to speak and stays silent, he becomes something dreadful. He is blind; he is a mute dog that cannot bark (Isaiah 56:10) — no better than those *outside* the flock, like the nations who do not even know what to look for or how to say it. The watchman who will not warn has turned against the very people of God he was set over; and his blindness invites the beasts of the field in to devour (Isaiah 56:9). To see and not speak is to fall out of the office altogether.

**The function — watch, guard, warn.** So the function stands plain: to watch, to guard, to see what comes against the city, and to warn. And even when the warning is refused, the word does not fail — Micah says *the day of your watchmen, your punishment, has come* (Micah 7:4): the day the watchman foretold you has arrived.

**Nehemiah — how the whole watch actually works.** Nehemiah gives the fullest, clearest picture of how the position works on the ground. Watch it move:
- The watch is set up when the people are **under attack** — when they are at war. (And notice: we are at war right now.)
- The watchmen **oversee and stand guard while the people build** — and the people build with a tool in one hand and a spear in the other, ready to fight at any moment (Nehemiah 4:16–18).
- The **trumpet gathers the people** — a rally to the fight wherever the enemy breaks in (Nehemiah 4:20).
- The watch runs **all the time**, so the watchmen **switch shifts** — and it is spread not only through *time* but through *distance*: every man has his own **post** along the wall (Nehemiah 7:3).
- The guard is set **even on the day of rest** — so that while the people rest, someone still stands watch (Nehemiah 13:19–22). The watch is never dropped; every man is ready for what comes.

**The revelation — the watch never stops, but the watchman can rest.** Here is the thing to see. Yes, there is always a watch — but there are **many** watchmen and **many** shifts. So the *watch* never stops, yet the individual *watchman* can. The watch is kept because it is the watch**men**, not the watch**man** — the watchman is one of a company, never a lone figure on the wall.

**Testimony — the restless watchman.** For the longest time I felt I had to keep watch for a very long time — that I could never come down off the wall. And the Lord told me: *you are the restless watchman, because you are afraid to let others take over the watch.* I had been seeking Him — how do I get others to watch? how would I ever have rest? — because You said in Your own word, "give Him no rest until He establishes Jerusalem and makes her a praise" (Isaiah 62:6–7); the watchman is to have no rest until You perform Your work. But then I understood: it is the **watch** that must never rest, not the **watchman**. The watchman is part of a body — we watch *for one another*; we do not watch alone. We take shifts. We trust one another to see what we ourselves would see if we were on the wall — and when any one watchman sees it and calls it out, the whole company is alerted to the same thing at once. That is how the watch never sleeps, and the watchmen still find their rest — all keep watch, so all may rest.

## D · Watching for the morning (expectancy)
The hope of the watch: the watchman endures the night because the morning is coming — and the morning is the very hour God acts (category E). Here watching *is* waiting, and waiting *is* hope.

> [!warning]- Disclaimer
> Admittedly, I have not drawn this section out as fully as the others — there is more here in the Word on the watch that waits in hope than what is set down. Take what is here as a beginning, and I encourage you to study it out for yourself; you will find more than what is written.

- Psalm 127:1 — "unless the LORD guards the city, the watchman stays awake in vain"
> [!note]- Helpful notes
> The psalm *of Solomon* (superscription). The watchman who gives God no rest (Isaiah 62:7) and refuses rest himself turns out to have his own rest in the Lord: his waking is vain unless the LORD is the true guard, and the next breath is rest — "He gives to His beloved even in his sleep" (Psalm 127:2). So Habakkuk, having taken his post, ends resting in God while all fails: "Though the fig tree does not blossom... yet I will exult in the LORD" (Habakkuk 3:17–18). The watchman keeps the watch, but the Lord keeps him.
- Psalm 130:6 — "my soul waits... more than the watchmen for the morning" — the soul's longing for the Lord pictured as the watchman's ache for dawn, and said twice over for its intensity; he waits because the morning is sure to come.
- Micah 7:7 — "I will watch expectantly for the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation" — the prophet's own turn: from the day of the judgment-watchmen (Micah 7:4, category C) to the hopeful watch, certain his God will hear.
- Lamentations 4:17 — "we kept watching for a nation that could not save" — the watch aimed at the wrong hope: they wore out their eyes looking to an ally that could not save, instead of watching for the LORD *(misplaced watching)*.

## E · The night watches (the frame of time)
Background vocabulary — the divisions of the night the watchman keeps.

> [!warning]- Disclaimer
> Admittedly, I do not have much revelation on the different timeframes of the watches. There is likely more depth here in the Word than what is set down — this simply was not an area I have been compelled to dig into, or been given much revelation on yet. So take this section as mainly informational: the background vocabulary of the watch, rather than a developed teaching.

> [!info]- Context — the count of the watches *(to be studied further)*
> The OT night runs in **three** watches: beginning (Lamentations 2:19), middle (Judges 7:19), morning (Exodus 14:24) — the word "**middle**" (Judges 7:19) proving three, not four, since a single centre is impossible in a fourfold division. The NT uses the later **Roman fourfold** system, named in full at Mark 13:35 (evening, midnight, cockcrow, morning). The mismatch is historical, not a textual gap: Israel kept three watches; the Roman era shortened them to four. *(Hours are approximate — the watches ran sunset to sunrise and drifted with the seasons; the number is firm, the clock is our gloss.)*

> [!note]- Helpful notes
> **The morning watch = the hour of deliverance.** The LORD routs Egypt "in the morning watch" (Exodus 14:24); Saul routs Ammon likewise (1 Samuel 11:11); Jesus comes on the water in the fourth/morning watch (Matthew 14:25). God acts in the last, darkest stretch before dawn — which is why the watchman waits for the morning (Psalm 130:6, category D).

- Exodus 14:24 — "the morning watch"
- Judges 7:19 — "the beginning of the middle watch" *(Gideon)*

#### The longer explanation
> *I hope you have studied the word above — in context — before reading this; you will get far more out of it.*

**Praying is keeping watch.** In the New Testament, praying *is* keeping watch. When you see something, you proclaim it — and when you see something, you pray. And your prayer is not one thing only: it can be a prayer to build up, or to break down; to come against the works of the enemy — for the prophet-watchman's own word was set "to pluck up and to break down... to build and to plant" (Jeremiah 1:10). Part of praying, too, is *waiting* — waiting on God to receive from Him. That is how you stay alert at all times: you pray, you wait, and you listen to hear what the Lord is saying to you. Watching and praying are one motion (Matthew 26:41; Luke 21:36; Ephesians 6:18).

**But there is also the action — proclaiming, as Paul did.** The watch is not prayer only; there is action too. Paul went out and preached — he was sent to speak of the Lord to people, to declare the gospel. That proclaiming is part of the watchman's assignment, and it belongs in your toolkit. It may not be your main thing — you may not be a runner — but it is still part of the watch: to open your mouth and say what you have seen.

**As an elder — keeping watch over souls.** And if you are set over others as a watchman — an overseer in the church — the watch becomes a watch over *souls* (Hebrews 13:17). This is why, all through the Scriptures, the word to the watchman is the same: be alert, be sober (1 Thessalonians 5:6; 1 Peter 5:8). But — as with everything here — you were never meant to keep it alone. You keep the watch as one of a company, as the watchmen together.

**The garment you never take off.** When we speak of the garment the watchman never takes off (Revelation 16:15; Nehemiah 4:23), we are not saying you may never rest. We are saying: keep your garment of righteousness on. Stay in right standing with the Lord. Keep your readiness — always have your oil, always have your fire, always be ready for the coming of the King (Matthew 25:1–13). Readiness is a garment you wear even while you rest.

**The collective keeps the watch.** Because the watchmen keep watch *together*, they keep one another informed. When one of them stays up and sees the works of the Lord — sees the good news — that word passes to the next watchman, and from him into the whole body. So you stay watchful for the coming of the King — and yet this does not mean there is no rest. The watch never sleeps *because* the watchmen are many; and so each of them, in his turn, may rest.

## Closing

The watchman is set inside the city to see and to speak — commissioned by the water, trained to hear how the Lord speaks, and charged to warn of judgement (A–B). He is the first to behold the works of the Lord, and he answers for the souls he watches over. Yet he never watches alone: the watch is a company, from Nehemiah's wall to the whole church (C–F). Not all are watchmen, but the watchmen are many — enough to man the walls, enough to watch through the night, and enough to take rest.
